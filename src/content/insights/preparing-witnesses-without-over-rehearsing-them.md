The single most common mistake in witness preparation is not under-preparation. It is over-rehearsal that produces a witness who sounds prepared in exactly the way that makes a jury distrust them.

## What juries actually notice

Jurors cannot always articulate what "coached" sounds like, but they recognize it reliably: answers that arrive too smoothly, phrasing that repeats verbatim across direct and cross, a witness who seems to be reciting rather than recalling. The paradox of trial preparation is that the witness who has been drilled the hardest often reads as the least credible.

## What preparation should actually accomplish

Preparation exists to do three things, and none of them involve scripting language.

**Familiarity with the process.** A witness who has never been in a courtroom is anxious about the format itself — where to sit, how to answer a compound question, what happens during an objection. Removing that anxiety is legitimate and important, and has nothing to do with the substance of their testimony.

**Accurate recall of documents.** If a witness is going to be asked about a document they last saw two years ago, they should see it again before they testify. Reviewing your own prior statements and the exhibits you will be shown is preparation, not coaching.

**An honest test of the hardest questions.** The most useful thing we do in preparation is ask the questions opposing counsel is most likely to ask, including the ones that go badly. A witness's first bad answer should happen in our conference room, not on cross-examination.

## Where the line sits

The test we use is simple: preparation helps a witness give their own accurate answer more clearly and confidently. Coaching replaces the witness's own answer with someone else's. If a witness cannot explain, in their own words, why they said what they said, the preparation went too far.

That distinction is not just an ethical one. Trial lawyers who blur it consistently produce worse witnesses, because a jury's trust, once lost in the first twenty minutes of cross-examination, rarely comes back for the rest of the testimony.
