Most governance problems we see are not caused by bad people. They are caused by decision-making habits that were correct for a five-person company and were never deliberately replaced as the company grew.

## The habits that stop working first

Three patterns show up more than any others.

**Verbal authority.** In the early days, a founder can approve a hire, a vendor contract and a pricing change in the same afternoon, informally, and everyone accepts it because everyone was in the room. Once the company has forty employees and a board, the same informal approval creates two problems at once: nobody downstream knows what was actually agreed, and the board has no record that the decision went through any process at all.

**Single-threaded knowledge.** If only one person understands why a material contract was structured the way it was, that contract is a liability the moment that person is unavailable — on a plane, in a dispute, or simply outvoted.

**Consensus without a tiebreaker.** Founding teams often decide everything by discussion until agreement. That works when the group is small and aligned. It stops working the first time two co-founders disagree about something consequential and there is no mechanism to resolve it other than continuing to argue.

## What we put in place instead

A written decision matrix, agreed once, that states plainly which decisions need board approval, which need a single executive's sign-off, and which can be made by anyone with budget authority — with dollar thresholds attached, not vague language like "significant" or "material."

A board that meets on a fixed schedule with a written pack circulated in advance, even when the news is uneventful. The discipline matters more in quiet quarters than difficult ones, because it is quiet quarters where the habit erodes first.

A documented conflicts-of-interest process for the transactions that will eventually involve a founder personally — a related-party lease, a loan from a director, an acquisition of a company a board member has a stake in. These transactions are not improper. What makes them a problem later is the absence of a contemporaneous record showing they were disclosed and independently approved at the time.

## When to do this

The honest answer is before you think you need to. The companies that come to us after a governance dispute almost always had the same conversation internally six months earlier and decided it could wait. By the time outside counsel is drafting a special committee resolution at 11pm before a signing, the options are worse and the fees are higher than they would have been for an afternoon of structuring work a year prior.

If your company has raised a priced round, added an outside board member, or grown past the point where you could list every material contract from memory, that is the point to have this conversation — not the point where a dispute forces it.
