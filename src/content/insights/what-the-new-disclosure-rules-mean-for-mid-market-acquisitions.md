Buyers in mid-market deals are asking for more in a disclosure schedule than they were three years ago, and asking earlier in the process. Sellers who treat disclosure as a task for the week before signing are consistently the ones who end up renegotiating price after diligence turns something up that a properly prepared schedule would have surfaced on their own terms.

## What has actually changed

The shift is less about a single new rule and more about buyers — and their counsel — calibrating to a stricter baseline across recent deals. In practice that means three things show up in almost every term sheet we negotiate now:

- **Earlier delivery.** Buyers increasingly want a draft disclosure schedule alongside the signed letter of intent, not four weeks into exclusivity.
- **Named exceptions, not general carve-outs.** "Except as set forth in the ordinary course of business" is being pushed back on. Buyers want the specific contract, the specific litigation, the specific IP assignment gap, named.
- **A live bring-down obligation.** Sellers are being asked to update the schedule continuously through closing, not just represent it was accurate as of signing.

## What this means for your timeline

If you are contemplating a sale in the next twelve months, the preparation work has moved earlier than most founders expect. We now tell clients to start a disclosure inventory before they engage a banker, not after a term sheet arrives — because the items that take longest to resolve (an unassigned patent, an oral agreement with a related party, an unresolved employee classification question) are exactly the ones that cannot be fixed in the two weeks before signing.

## The seller's advantage in getting ahead of it

Sellers sometimes treat thorough disclosure as a concession to the buyer. It is closer to the opposite. A schedule you control, prepared on your own timeline with your own counsel, lets you fix what can be fixed and frame what cannot, before a buyer's diligence team finds it first and prices it into a retrading conversation.

We build disclosure preparation into every sell-side engagement as a discrete first phase, priced and scoped separately from the transaction itself, specifically so it happens before a letter of intent creates timeline pressure to skip it.
